**The Keeper and the Shell** — a short film, about two minutes. No dialogue, just score.

Dawn comes in grey and rose over a cold northern shore. The sea is quiet, folding in long slow waves that hiss across dark sand. Above the beach, on a spur of black rock, stands the lighthouse — white paint weathered to the color of bone, its lamp room dim now that the night is over. Everything looks as though it were painted in oils: soft edges, heavy skies, light that seems to come from inside the clouds.

The keeper walks the tideline as she does every morning. She is in her fifties, wrapped in a long wool coat, a knitted scarf pulled up against the wind, grey hair loose at her temples. She moves slowly, carefully, a person used to being alone. The score begins with a single cello, low and patient.

Among the seaweed and broken driftwood, something glows. She stops. Half-buried in the wet sand lies a spiral shell, pale as pearl, and inside its curve there is a soft light — not reflected, but its own, a faint blue-gold pulse like a heartbeat. She kneels and lifts it. The glow warms her cupped hands. She looks out at the sea for a long moment, as if asking permission, and then she slips the shell into her coat pocket.

Inside the keeper's cottage at the foot of the tower, the morning is plain and small: a kettle, a single chair, a table with a logbook and a cracked cup. She sets the shell on the windowsill. Its light is fainter here, away from the water. She watches it while she drinks her tea, and the strings gather into a gentle, uncertain melody.

Night falls. Wind rises off the sea, and the first rain strikes the window. The great lamp turns above the cottage, its beam sweeping out across the black water. On the windowsill, the shell begins to hum — a low, resonant tone that blends into the score until it is hard to tell which is which. Its glow brightens and dims in long, slow waves. The keeper wakes in her chair and turns toward it.

Then the beam above her stutters. It flickers once, twice, and goes out. The tower stands dark against a sky now torn by storm. Far out on the water, small lights rock in the swell — ships, three of them, struggling toward a coast they can no longer see.

She does not hesitate. She takes the shell from the sill, pulls on her coat, and runs out into the rain. The storm is enormous: wind tearing at her scarf, waves exploding white against the rocks, lightning flaring the whole sea silver for an instant and then plunging it back into black. She pushes open the iron door of the tower and climbs. The spiral stairs wind up and up, slick with water, the shell glowing brighter in her hand with every turn, its hum rising with the music — drums now, deep and urgent, under the strings.

At the top, the lamp room is cold and silent. The great lens sits dead in its brass frame. She holds the shell up to it. For a breath, nothing. Then the shell's light pours out of its spiral and into the glass, flooding the lens with blue-gold fire. The lamp roars back to life. The beam sweeps out over the storm, broader and warmer than it has ever been, cutting a path of gold across the waves.

Out at sea, the ships turn. One by one, they find the beam and steer away from the rocks, their small lights sliding safely past the point and on toward the harbor. The keeper stands at the glass, soaked and shaking, watching until the last of them is clear. The score swells, then softens into a single sustained note.

Morning again. The storm has passed, and the sky is washed pale and clean. The sea lies calm. The keeper walks down to the beach, the shell in her hands. Its glow is very faint now, tired, as if it gave almost everything it had. She kneels at the same spot on the tideline where she found it and sets it gently in the wet sand. A small wave runs up and wraps around it, then pulls back.

She stays kneeling for a moment, then rises and walks back toward the lighthouse without looking back. Behind her, the shell's light pulses once, softly, and the next wave carries it away. The cello plays its first melody again, alone, and the painted dawn fades to white.
